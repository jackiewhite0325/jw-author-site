From chapter one · the weight of rupture

The apartment did not breathe; it merely held its breath.

Clara stood in the narrow corridor of the north London flat, her fingers hooked into the top edge of a double-walled cardboard moving box that had remained unsealed on the parquet floor for exactly six months. It sat there like an unburied bone, a stubborn spatial anomaly in a room that Julian had otherwise curated with the terrifying, sterile precision of a high-end gallery. Inside the box, wrapped in sheets of yellowed newsprint from newspapers whose headlines had long since ceased to matter, were the remnants of a life that felt increasingly like fiction: her father's heavy brass fountain pens, his academic journals on structural linguistics, and a thick, blue plastic folder containing the unfiled medical invoices from his final twenty-eight days in the hospice.

Loss, Clara had discovered during this long, grey decade of erosion, was not a sharp instrument. It did not slice through a life with the clean, dramatic velocity that stories promised. It was a heavy, atmospheric low-pressure system that settled into the corners of a room, slowing the transit of dust motes in the pale afternoon light, coating the drywall like an invisible, industrial grease. It was a tax paid in minutes: the extra three seconds it took to lift a cast-iron kettle, the long, hollow vacuum between dialing a familiar number and remembering there was no one on the other end to pick up the receiver. It was the crushing, wordless weight of realizing she was the last one left to carry the quiet house.

On the mantelpiece sat two identical antique pocket watches, their silver-plated casings tarnished to the color of wet slate. They had belonged to her grandfather, a watchmaker from Warsaw, then to her father, and now they sat side by side, completely unwound. Their brass hands were frozen at entirely different, meaningless coordinates: one at four minutes past twelve, the other at twenty-three minutes to eight. Clara stared at them, her pale reflection caught in the dull, concave glass of the casings.
